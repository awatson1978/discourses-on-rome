The Fragments of Lost Livy

Verbatim survivals of the 107 lost books of Ab Urbe Condita — a dossier of the Lost Books Program · github.com/awatson1978/discourses-on-rome · 2026-11-17

Of Livy's 142 books, 107 are lost. Their content survives in summaries and epitomes — the Periochae, Obsequens, Florus, Orosius — but their words survive in only a handful of places: one physical palimpsest, and quotations by later authors who name their source. This dossier lists every verbatim fragment known to this project. Rule of the registry: fragment text enters only from an acquired, cited witness text, machine-validated as a verbatim substring; summaries, paraphrases, and reconstructions are never fragments.

Book 120 — Livy's narrative of Cicero's death (43 BCE), quoted verbatim and attributed by Seneca; the.

Fragment: frag:b120:seneca-suasoriae-6-17
Transmission: quotation with attribution
Witness: Seneca the Elder, Suasoriae 6.17
Status: ACQUIRED — verbatim from wit:seneca:suasoriae (The Latin Library), the block Seneca introduces 'ita enim ait: T. LIVI.'; validated as substring of the witness record.
M. Cicero sub adventum triumvirorum urbe cesserat pro certo habens, id quod erat, non magis Antonio (se) eripi quam Caesari Cassium et Brutum posse. primo in Tusculanum fugerat, inde transversis itineribus in Formianum ut ab Caieta navem conscensurus proficiscitur. unde aliquotiens in altum provectum cum modo venti adversi rettulissent, modo ipse iactationem navis caeco volvente fluctu pati non posset, taedium tandem eum et fugae et vitae cepit regressusque ad superiorem villam, quae paulo plus mille passibus a mari abest, 'moriar' inquit 'in patria saepe servata'. satis constat servos fortiter fideliterque paratos fuisse ad dimicandum; ipsum deponi lecticam et quietos pati quod fors iniqua cogeret iussisse. prominenti ex lectica praebentique immotam cervicem caput praecisum est. nec satis stolidae crudelitati militum fuit: manus quoque scripsisse aliquid in Antonium exprobrantes praeciderunt. ita relatum caput ad Antonium iussuque eius inter duas manus in rostris positum, ubi ille consul, ubi saepe consularis, ubi eo ipso anno adversus Antonium quanta nulla umquam humana vox cum admiratione eloquentiae auditus fuerat. vix attollentes (madentes) lacrimis oculos homines intueri trucidati membra civis poterant.
Working translation — this project (AI-assisted, unreviewed); the Latin is the authority
Marcus Cicero had left the city shortly before the arrival of the triumvirs, holding it for certain — as was true — that he could no more be snatched from Antony than Cassius and Brutus from Caesar. First he had fled to his Tusculan estate; from there he set out by cross-roads to his villa at Formiae, intending to board a ship at Caieta. Having put out to sea several times, when contrary winds now carried him back, and now he himself could not endure the tossing of the ship as the blind swell rolled it, weariness at last overcame him — of flight and of life alike — and returning to his upper villa, which stands a little more than a mile from the sea, he said: "I shall die in the fatherland I have so often saved." It is well established that his slaves were ready to fight bravely and faithfully; it was he who ordered them to set the litter down and to suffer quietly what an unjust fortune compelled. As he leaned from the litter and offered his neck unmoving, his head was struck off. Nor was that enough for the soldiers' doltish cruelty: they cut off the hands too, reproaching them for having written something against Antony. So the head was carried back to Antony and by his order set between the two hands on the Rostra — where as consul, where so often as an ex-consul, where in that very year against Antony, he had been heard with such admiration of his eloquence as no human voice ever earned. Scarcely lifting eyes wet with tears, men could not look upon the butchered limbs of their fellow citizen.

Book 120 — Livy's formal verdict on Cicero (the 'vir magnus ac memorabilis' judgment), quoted with the T.

Fragment: frag:b120:seneca-suasoriae-6-22
Transmission: quotation with attribution
Witness: Seneca the Elder, Suasoriae 6.22 (wit:seneca:suasoriae)
Status: ACQUIRED — verbatim from wit:seneca:suasoriae; validated as substring of the witness record.
Vixit tres et sexaginta annos, ut, si vis afuisset, ne immatura quidem mors videri possit. ingenium et operibus et praemiis operum felix, ipse fortunae diu prosperae; sed in longo tenore felicitatis magnis interim ictus vulneribus, exilio, ruina partium, pro quibus steterat, filiae amatae exitu tam tristi atque acerbo, omnium adversorum nihil, ut viro dignum erat, tulit praeter mortem, quae vere aestimanti minus indigna videri potuit, quod a victore inimico (nihil) crudelius passus erat quam quod eiusdem fortunae compos victo fecisset. si quis tamen virtutibus vitia pensarit, vir magnus ac memorabilis fuit, et in cuius laudes (ex)equendas Cicerone laudatore opus fuerit.
Working translation — this project (AI-assisted, unreviewed); the Latin is the authority
He lived sixty-three years, so that even had no violence intervened, his death could not seem untimely. His genius was fortunate in its works and in their rewards; he himself long enjoyed prosperous fortune; but amid that long course of happiness he was struck at intervals by heavy wounds — exile, the ruin of the party he had stood for, the death of a beloved daughter so sad and bitter — and of all his adversities he bore none as became a man except his death, which, weighed truly, could seem the less undeserved in that he suffered from his victorious enemy nothing more cruel than he would have done, had he enjoyed the same fortune, to the vanquished. Yet if one weighs his virtues against his faults, he was a great and memorable man — and to tell out his praises, one would need a Cicero for the eulogist.
