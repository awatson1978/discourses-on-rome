*Era: 167–91 BCE — post-Pydna Mediterranean hegemony, Carthage and Corinth destroyed, the Gracchi, Jugurtha, Cimbri, the road to the Social War.*

The Numantines, driven by hunger, killed one another in turn; Scipio Africanus destroyed the captured city, and triumphed over it in the fourteenth year after Carthage had been destroyed [59.1]. The consul P. Rupilius finished the war with the runaway slaves in Sicily [59.2]. Aristonicus, a son of king Eumenes, seized Asia, though by the testament of king Attalus it had been left to the Roman people, that it should be free [59.3]. The consul P. Licinius Crassus, though he was also pontifex maximus—a thing never before done—set out beyond Italy against Aristonicus; he was defeated in battle and killed [59.4]. The consul M. Perperna defeated Aristonicus and received his surrender [59.5]. Q. Pompeius and Q. Metellus, then for the first time both censors from the plebs, closed the lustrum; the heads of citizens counted were three hundred and eighteen thousand eight hundred and twenty-three, not counting orphan boys, orphan girls, and widows [59.6]. The censor Q. Metellus proposed that all men be compelled to take wives, for the begetting of children; his speech is extant, which Augustus Caesar read in the senate, as though written for his own times, when he dealt with the marriage of the orders [59.7]. The tribune of the plebs C. Atinius Labeo ordered the censor Q. Metellus, by whom he had been passed over in the revision of the senate, to be thrown from the rock; but the other tribunes came to the rescue [59.8]. When the tribune Carbo proposed that it be lawful to make the same man tribune as often as one wished, P. Africanus opposed the bill in a weighty speech, in which he said that Ti. Gracchus seemed to him justly slain; C. Gracchus spoke for the proposal, but Scipio prevailed [59.9]. The wars waged between Antiochus of Syria and Phraates, king of the Parthians, are set forth, and the affairs of Egypt, no quieter [59.10]. Ptolemy, surnamed Euergetes, hated by his own people for his excessive cruelty, fled secretly to Cyprus when the mob set fire to his palace; and when the kingdom was given to his sister Cleopatra, he killed the son whom he had had by her, and sent the head, the hands, and the feet to the mother [59.11]. Seditions were stirred up by the triumvirs Fulvius Flaccus, C. Gracchus, and C. Papirius Carbo, created for dividing the land [59.12]. P. Scipio Africanus, who opposed them, having returned home hale and strong the day before, was found dead in his bedroom; his wife Sempronia, sister of the Gracchi, was held in suspicion of poison, yet no inquiry was held into his death [59.13]. The consul C. Sempronius at first fared ill against the Iapydes, but the defeat was soon redeemed by a victory, won through the valour of Decimus Junius Brutus, the conqueror of Lusitania [59.14].
