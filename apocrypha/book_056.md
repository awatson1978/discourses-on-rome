Decimus Junius Brutus fought with success against the Gallaeci in Further Spain [56.1]. With unlike fortune the proconsul M. Aemilius Lepidus campaigned against the Vaccaei, and suffered a disaster like that at Numantia [56.2]. To release the people from the religious bond of the Numantine treaty, Mancinus, its author, was handed over to the Numantines, who would not receive him [56.3]. And since the Numantine war dragged on, through the fault of its commanders, not without public shame, the consulship was conferred unsought upon Scipio Africanus by the senate and the Roman people; and he was again exempted from the law which forbade a second consulship [56.7]. A slave war arose in Sicily, begun by Eunus, a slave of Syrian birth, who gathered a band of rural slaves, opened the slave-prisons, and made up the numbers of a regular army; and when the praetors could not suppress it, the war was entrusted to the consul C. Fulvius [56.8]. Cleon, a second slave, gathered as many as seventy thousand slaves; and joining their forces, they waged war again and again against the Roman army [56.9].
