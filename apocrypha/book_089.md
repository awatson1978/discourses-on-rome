Marcus Brutus, sent by Gnaeus Papirius Carbo from Cossyra in a fishing boat to Lilybaeum, to learn whether Pompeius was there, was surrounded by Pompeius's ships, and killed himself, falling upon his sword set against a thwart of the ship [89.1]. Gnaeus Pompeius, sent into Sicily with imperium by the senate, captured Gnaeus Carbo and put him to death — and Carbo bore his death weeping like a woman [89.2].

Sulla was made dictator, and proceeded with twenty-four fasces, which no man had ever done [89.3]. He confirmed the condition of the state with new laws: the power of the tribunes of the plebs he diminished, and took from them the whole right of proposing laws [89.4]; the colleges of the pontiffs and of the augurs he enlarged, so that each had fifteen members [89.5]; the senate he filled up from the equestrian order [89.6]. The children of the proscribed he stripped of the right to seek office, and their goods he sold, seizing much for himself; the sum realized was three hundred and fifty million sesterces [89.7]. Quintus Lucretius Afella, because he dared to seek the consulship against Sulla's will, Sulla ordered killed in the forum; and when the Roman people, assembled, took it badly, he told them that he himself had ordered it [89.8].

Gnaeus Pompeius in Africa defeated and killed the proscribed Gnaeus Domitius and Hierta, king of Numidia, who were stirring up war [89.9]; and at twenty-four years of age, while still a Roman knight — which had fallen to no man before — he triumphed from Africa [89.10].

Gaius Norbanus, an ex-consul and proscribed, killed himself in the city of Rhodes when he was about to be apprehended [89.11]. Mutilus, one of the proscribed, came secretly, his head covered, to the back door of his wife Bastia; refused admission because, as she said, he was a proscribed man, he stabbed himself and spattered her doors with his blood [89.12].

Sulla recovered Aesernia in Samnium [89.13]. On the captured lands he settled forty-seven legions, and divided the lands among them [89.14]. Volaterrae, a town still in arms, he besieged and received in surrender [89.15]. Mytilene in Asia, the one city that still retained its arms after the defeat of Mithridates, was stormed and destroyed [89.16].
