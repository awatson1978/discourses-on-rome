*Era: 167–91 BCE — post-Pydna Mediterranean hegemony, Carthage and Corinth destroyed, the Gracchi, Jugurtha, Cimbri, the road to the Social War.*

The Third Punic War began in the six hundred and second year from the founding of the city, and within the fifth year of its beginning was brought to an end [49.1]. M. Porcius Cato and Scipio Nasica contended with opposing opinions, Cato urging war and that Carthage be removed and destroyed, Nasica arguing against it [49.2]. It was resolved to declare war upon the Carthaginians: because they had ships contrary to the treaty, because they had led an army outside their borders, because they had borne arms against Masinissa, an ally and friend of the Roman people, and because they had refused to receive his son Gulussa into their city [49.3]. Before any forces had been embarked, envoys from Utica came to Rome, surrendering themselves and all that they had—an omen welcome to the fathers, and bitter to the Carthaginians [49.4]. Games were held to Dis Pater at Tarentum, in accordance with the Sibylline books; they had last been held a hundred years before, in the First Punic War, in the five hundred and second year of the city [49.5]. Thirty envoys came to Rome, through whom the Carthaginians surrendered themselves; but the opinion of Cato prevailed, that the decree should stand, and that the consuls should set out to war with all speed [49.6]. The consuls, when they had received at Carthage three hundred hostages and all the arms and instruments of war that were there, ordered on the authority of the senate that the Carthaginians build their town in another place, no less than ten miles from the sea; and by this indignity they drove the Carthaginians to war [49.7]. Carthage began to be besieged and assaulted by the consuls L. Marcius and M'. Manilius [49.8]. In the assault Scipio extricated two tribunes who had rashly broken in with their cohorts; he freed a Roman fort attacked by night, with the help of a few horsemen; and for the relief of the besieged camp he won the chief glory [49.9]. And when the consul's army had been routed by Hasdrubal in an unfavourable pass—against which Scipio had given warning—he returned with a few squadrons of horse, freed the two cohorts that were besieged, and brought them back unharmed [49.10]. Cato declared in the senate that the others serving in Africa flitted about as shadows, while Scipio was full of vigour; and the Roman people so embraced him that most of the tribes voted him consul, though his years did not permit it [49.11]. When the tribune L. Scribonius proposed that the Lusitanians whom Ser. Galba had sold into slavery in Gaul be restored to freedom, Cato pressed the measure fiercely; but Galba, pleading pitiably as he embraced his young sons and his ward, secured its rejection [49.12]. Andriscus, a man of the lowest station, giving himself out as Philippus and a son of king Perseus, escaped secretly from Rome, gathered an army about his invented tale of royal birth, and occupied all Macedonia, by consent or by arms [49.13].
