*Era: 292–219 BCE — Third Samnite aftermath, Pyrrhic War, First Punic War, interwar years.*

The consul Fabius Gurges fought without success against the Samnites; and when the senate deliberated upon removing him from his army, his father Fabius Maximus turned aside the disgrace, moving the fathers above all by his promise to go as legate to his own son, which promise he kept [11.1]. Aided by the old man's counsel and service, the consul cut the Samnites to pieces and triumphed; C. Pontius, commander of the Samnites, was led in the triumph and beheaded [11.2]. When the city laboured under pestilence, envoys were sent to bring from Epidaurus the serpent in which the godhead of Aesculapius was held to reside; and where it went ashore, upon the island of the Tiber, a temple to Aesculapius was established [11.3]. L. Postumius, a man of consular rank, was condemned because, while he commanded an army, he had used the labour of his soldiers upon his own land [11.4]. The Samnites asked for peace, and the treaty with them was renewed for the fourth time [11.5]. The consul Curius Dentatus slaughtered the Samnites, defeated the Sabines who had rebelled and received their surrender, and twice triumphed in the same magistracy [11.6]. Colonies were led out to Castrum, to Sena, and to Hadria [11.7]. Then for the first time triumviri capitales were created [11.8]. The census was held and the lustrum closed; the heads of citizens counted were two hundred and seventy-two thousand [11.9]. The plebs, on account of debt, after seditions grave and long, seceded at the last to the Janiculum; thence they were led back by the dictator Q. Hortensius, who died in that very magistracy [11.10]. Campaigns besides were waged against the Vulsinienses, and against the Lucanians, against whom it had been resolved to bring aid to the Thurians [11.11].
