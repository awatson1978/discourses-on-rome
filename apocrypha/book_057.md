Scipio Africanus laid siege to Numantia, and recalled the army, corrupted by licence and luxury, to the strictest military discipline, cutting away every instrument of pleasure and driving two thousand prostitutes from the camp [57.1]. Daily the soldier was kept at work, and compelled to carry grain for thirty days and seven stakes besides; and the man who straggled under his load Scipio rebuked with sharp sayings [57.2]. Any soldier caught out of his rank he beat—with vine-rods if he were a Roman, with staves if a foreigner; and he sold all the pack animals, lest they relieve the soldiers of their loads [57.3]. Against the sorties of the enemy he fought often and with success [57.4]. The Vaccaei, under siege, slaughtered their children and their wives, and killed themselves [57.5]. The lavish gifts sent to Scipio by Antiochus, king of Syria, he declared he would accept openly, before the tribunal; he ordered the quaestor to enter them in the public records, and said that from them he would give gifts to brave men [57.6]. Having shut in Numantia on every side, and seeing the besieged pressed by hunger, he forbade the killing of enemies who went out to forage, saying that the more they were, the faster they would consume what grain they had [57.7].
