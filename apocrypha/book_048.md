The lustrum was closed by the censors; the heads of citizens counted were three hundred and twenty-four thousand [48.1]. The seeds of the Third Punic War are set forth [48.2]. When a great Numidian army under Ariobarzanes was reported to be in Carthaginian territory, M. Porcius Cato urged that war be declared upon Carthage; but P. Cornelius Nasica speaking against it, it was resolved to send envoys to Carthage, to observe [48.3]. The envoys, attempting to make peace between Carthage and Masinissa, stood in danger of their lives when Gisgo the son of Hamilcar, a magistrate, stirred up the people to war against Rome; and they escaped violence only by flight [48.4]. M. Porcius Cato, being a poor man, gave his son, who died in his praetorship, a funeral at the least possible expense [48.5]. Andriscus, who with enormous persistence falsely gave himself out to be the son of Perseus, once king of Macedonia, was sent to Rome [48.6]. M. Aemilius Lepidus, whom six successive pairs of censors had chosen princeps senatus, before he died charged his sons to conduct his funeral modestly, since the funerals of great men were ennobled by the images of ancestors, not by expense [48.7]. An inquiry into poisonings was held: the noblewomen Publilia and Licinia, accused of killing their husbands, men of consular rank, were, after the case was heard, put to death by decree of their kinsmen [48.8]. Gulussa, the son of Masinissa, reported that at Carthage levies were being held, a fleet made ready, and war beyond doubt contrived; it was resolved that ten envoys be sent to investigate [48.9]. The consuls L. Licinius Lucullus and A. Postumius Albinus, conducting the levy strictly and exempting no man by favour, were thrown into prison by the tribunes of the plebs [48.10]. And when the ill success of the war in Spain so dismayed the citizens that none would go as tribune or as legate, P. Cornelius Aemilianus came forward and declared that he would accept whatever kind of military service was given him; by which example he roused all to enthusiasm for service [48.11]. The consul Lucullus subdued the Vaccaei, the Cantabri, and other peoples of Spain until then unknown [48.12]. There P. Cornelius Scipio Aemilianus, serving as tribune of the soldiers, killed a barbarian who had challenged him; and at the storming of the city of Intercatia he ran still greater danger, being the first to mount the wall [48.13]. The praetor Ser. Sulpicius Galba fought ill against the Lusitanians [48.14]. When the envoys returned from Africa, reporting an army and a fleet at Carthage, the senate resolved to abstain from war if the Carthaginians would burn their fleet and dismiss their army; otherwise the next consuls should put the question of a Punic war [48.15]. A theatre, contracted for by the censors, was, on the motion of P. Cornelius Nasica, destroyed by decree of the senate as useless and harmful to public morals; and the people for some time watched the games standing [48.16]. The Carthaginians made war upon Masinissa contrary to the treaty; they were defeated by him—a man then two and ninety years of age—and incurred besides the war with Rome [48.17]. Disturbances in Syria, and the wars waged among its kings, are set forth; in the course of which Demetrius, king of Syria, was killed [48.18].
