The panic of the defeated party, and its flight into the different parts of the world, are here related [112.1]. Gnaeus Pompeius, making for Egypt, was killed by Achillas before he could land, in a small boat, by order of king Ptolemy, his own ward, at the urging of the tutor Theodotus and of Pothinus [112.2]. His wife Cornelia and his son Sextus Pompeius fled to Cyprus [112.3].

Caesar, following after on the third day, when Theodotus offered him the head of Pompeius and his ring, was moved to anger and to tears; and he entered Alexandria, then in riot, without danger [112.4]. Created dictator, he restored Cleopatra to the kingdom of Egypt, and at great hazard defeated Ptolemy, who had made war upon him at the urging of the same men by whose counsel Pompeius had been killed; the king, fleeing, sank in a small boat in the Nile [112.5].

This book also contains the laborious march of Marcus Cato, who led his legions through the deserts of Africa [112.6]. A war was waged by Gnaeus Domitius against Pharnaces with little success [112.7].
