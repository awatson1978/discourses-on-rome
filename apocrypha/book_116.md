Caesar celebrated a fifth triumph, from Spain [116.1]. Honors very many and very great were decreed to him by the senate, among them that he be called father of his country, and that he be sacrosanct, and dictator in perpetuity [116.2]. Yet ill-will he incurred: because he did not rise for the senate as it conferred these honors upon him; because, when the consul Marcus Antonius, running among the Luperci, set a diadem upon his head, he placed it upon his chair; and because the tribunes Epidius Marullus and Caesetius Flavus, for stirring odium against him as one aspiring to kingship, were stripped of their power [116.3].

Against him a conspiracy was formed, its heads Marcus Brutus and Gaius Cassius, and, from Caesar's own party, Decimus Brutus and Gaius Trebonius; and in the senate-house of Pompeius he was killed, with twenty-three wounds [116.4]. The Capitol was occupied by his assassins; and when the senate had decreed oblivion for the killing, and hostages had been received from the children of Antonius and of Lepidus, the conspirators came down from the Capitol [116.5]. By Caesar's will Gaius Octavius, his sister's grandson, was instituted heir to half his estate and adopted into his name [116.6]. While the body was being borne to the Campus Martius, it was cremated by the plebs before the rostra [116.7].

The honor of the dictatorship was abolished in perpetuity [116.8]. Chamates, a man of the lowest station, who gave himself out to be the son of Gaius Marius, was put to death for stirring seditions among the credulous plebs [116.9].
