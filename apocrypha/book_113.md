The Pompeian party having drawn itself together in Africa, its command was conferred upon Publius Scipio, Cato yielding place although the command had been offered to him on equal terms [113.1]. And when the destruction of the city of Utica was debated on account of its goodwill toward Caesar, and Marcus Cato had prevented it, the protection and custody of that city were entrusted to Cato himself [113.2].

In Spain, Gnaeus Pompeius, son of Magnus, gathered forces — a command which neither Afranius nor Petreius would take up — and renewed the war against Caesar [113.3]. Pharnaces, son of Mithridates and king of Pontus, was defeated without any delay of war [113.4].

At Rome, when seditions had been stirred by the tribune of the plebs Publius Dolabella, who proposed a law for the cancellation of debts, and the plebs rioted, soldiers were brought into the city by Marcus Antonius, master of the horse, and eight hundred of the plebs were killed [113.5]. To the veterans who demanded their discharge with mutiny Caesar granted it [113.6]. Then, crossing to Africa, he fought at great hazard against the forces of king Juba [113.7].
